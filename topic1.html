1. Causes and Major Drivers of Forest Fires

Forest fires, also called wildfires, can start from both natural and human causes. Natural causes include lightning, extreme heat and, in some ecosystems, volcanic activity. However, human activities are responsible for a large share of wildfire ignitions in many regions. These include agricultural and land-clearing fires, unattended campfires, discarded cigarettes, machinery and power-line failures, burning of vegetation, and deliberate ignition.

The exact proportion varies considerably by region. For example, the National Park Service (NPS) estimates that nearly 85% of wildland fires in the United States were human-caused during 2000–2017. At the global level, the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) reports that humans cause at least 90% of wildfires, although the causes and proportions differ between countries and ecosystems.

The conditions after ignition are also important: drought, high temperatures, strong winds and dry vegetation can allow a small fire to spread rapidly. Climate change can increase the occurrence of some of these favourable fire conditions.

Source: Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), National Park Service (NPS)
